An ERP project that is over budget rarely fails in one moment. It drifts: a change here, a delay there, data that takes longer than planned, a go-live date that moves again. By the time someone asks what went wrong, the money is spent and the team is tired. The good news is that most troubled ERP projects can be recovered. This guide is the recovery plan we use.

## Step 1: Stop the overrun

Before fixing anything, stop making it bigger.

- **Freeze new scope.** No new requirements enter the project until it is re-baselined. Good ideas go on a written list for later.
- **Pause custom development** that is not essential for going live.
- **Keep the data and configuration work going.** Those are needed whatever happens next.

## Step 2: Find out where the budget went

Overruns feel general, but they have specific causes. Go through time and cost by area:

| Area | What overrun usually means |
|---|---|
| Customisation | The software is being made to copy the old process |
| Data migration | The data was dirtier than planned, or cleanup started late |
| Rework | Requirements changed after configuration, or were never written down |
| Waiting | Decisions on the client side took days or weeks |
| Integrations | Systems were added that were never in the scope |

The pattern tells you what to fix. If most of the money went into customisation, the fix is scope. If it went into waiting, the fix is decision-making.

## Step 3: Re-baseline phase one

Write a new, smaller phase one that can go live:

- Core processes only: usually sales, purchasing, inventory, invoicing and accounting.
- Standard flows wherever the software already does the job.
- A clear definition of done for each process, agreed in writing.
- Everything else moved to a dated phase-two backlog, with an estimate.

A smaller phase one that goes live beats a complete phase one that never does. Once the business runs on the system, phase two is far easier to plan.

## Step 4: Fix the data in parallel

Data is the most common hidden cause of delay. Give it its own workstream with a template, an owner on your side and a verification step. Migrate only what the new system needs: open balances and active records, not ten years of history.

## Step 5: Put decisions back on a schedule

Name one decision-maker on each side with the authority to decide, and hold a weekly decision meeting. Anything that waits longer than a week goes to a sponsor. Slow decisions are one of the most expensive, least visible causes of overruns.

## Step 6: Train before go-live, by role

Projects in trouble often cut training. That makes the first month after go-live worse. Train each role on its own processes with its own data before cut-over.

## Warning signs a project needs a rescue now

- The go-live date has moved more than twice.
- Nobody can say exactly what phase one must do.
- Change requests outnumber completed tasks.
- Users are still keeping the old spreadsheets "just in case".
- The partner and the business describe the problem differently.
