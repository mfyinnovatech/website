This case study looks at a US distributor that outgrew QuickBooks and spreadsheets. The client is not named; the figures are the ones already published on our founder's profile.

## The project at a glance

| | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Systems | QuickBooks, spreadsheets and one person's memory | One ERP for inventory, purchasing, manufacturing and accounting |
| Warehouses and catalogue | 4 warehouses, more than 3,000 SKUs | Same, with stock visible in one place |
| Stock-outs | 2–3 a week | Near zero within two months |
| Purchase orders | Raised by hand | 90% generated by reorder rules |
| Month-end close | 4 days | Half a day |
| Time to go live | – | 10 weeks |
| Payback | – | By month 4 |

## The situation

The company had 4 warehouses and more than 3,000 SKUs, and its tools had not kept up:

- **Accounting** ran in QuickBooks.
- **Stock** was tracked in spreadsheets.
- **Reordering** depended on one person's memory.

The result: products ran out two or three times a week, and closing the books took four days every month.

## What the implementation covered

The ERP implementation covered **inventory, purchasing and manufacturing**.

- **Stock in one place.** All 4 warehouses and more than 3,000 SKUs in one system, with real-time quantities instead of spreadsheets.
- **Reorder rules instead of memory.** Minimum and maximum stock levels and vendor lead times let the system generate purchase orders when stock runs low. Today 90% of purchase orders are generated this way.
- **Small settings over custom code.** A reorder rule set up in minutes can save more time than a new custom module, and it keeps future upgrades simple.

## The results

- **Stock-outs** fell from 2–3 a week to **near zero within two months**.
- **Reorder rules** now generate **90% of purchase orders** automatically.
- **Month-end close** went from **4 days to half a day**.
- The project **went live in 10 weeks** and **paid for itself by month 4**.

## What other distributors can take from this

1. **Start where it hurts most.** Go live with the area in the most pain, make it stable, and let the team win once before adding the next part.
2. **Judge an ERP on your worst order, not a demo.** Ask vendors to run last month's messiest order with your data.
3. **Replace memory with rules.** If reordering depends on one person, it will fail the week they are away.
4. **Clean the data first.** Fixing product codes, units and opening stock is cheaper before go-live than after; messy data is the most common reason ERP timelines slip (see [how long an ERP implementation really takes](/blog/how-long-does-an-erp-implementation-take)).
5. **Watch month-end.** If close takes days because stock values do not reconcile, the problem is the system, not the accountants.
