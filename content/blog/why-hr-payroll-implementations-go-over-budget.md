HR and payroll projects look simple on a proposal: a list of modules, a licence price and an implementation fee. Then the project starts, and the real work appears. Employee files that disagree with each other. Leave balances nobody can explain. A payroll rule that only one person understands. This guide covers why HR and payroll implementations go over budget, in the US, in Kuwait and anywhere else, and what to do so yours does not.

## 1. The budget covers the licence, not the project

Most HR budgets are built from the vendor quote. The quote covers software and the partner's hours. It does not cover the work your own people must do: cleaning data, making policy decisions, testing payroll, attending training and running the old process in parallel. That internal effort is real cost. When it was never planned, it shows up as delays, overtime and change requests.

**Fix:** budget the project, not the licence. List the internal tasks next to the partner's tasks, with named owners and hours.

## 2. Employee data is messier than anyone admits

Duplicate employees, missing contract dates, job titles typed five different ways, leave balances carried forward by hand. HR data lives in spreadsheets, old payroll files and email, and none of it agrees. Data cleanup is routinely estimated as a week and turns into the longest task in the project.

**Fix:** start data cleanup in week one, with a migration template, an owner on your side and a verification step. Migrate only what the new system needs to run.

## 3. Integrations nobody priced

Payroll has to post to the general ledger. Attendance devices have to feed working hours. Banks need payment files. Benefits and insurance providers need data. Each integration is a small project with its own testing, and each one missing from the scope becomes a change request.

**Fix:** list every system HR and payroll must talk to before signing, and price each integration explicitly. If finance already runs on an ERP, consider running HR and payroll inside it: one integration fewer, and payroll costs land directly on the right cost centres.

## 4. Payroll rules are local and full of exceptions

Payroll is where general software meets local law.

- **In the United States**, federal and state withholding, benefits deductions and paid leave policies differ by state and by employee, and multi-state teams multiply the rules.
- **In Kuwait**, payroll needs end-of-service indemnity accruals (15 days' pay per year for the first five years, then one month's pay per year), leave entitlements and PIFSS social security contributions for Kuwaiti nationals. Contribution rates change, so confirm current figures with PIFSS or your advisor.

Every exception your company has, such as allowances, overtime rules or special contracts, is configuration and testing time.

**Fix:** document your payroll rules and exceptions before configuration starts, and have someone from payroll sign them off.

## 5. HR and IT people are only part-time on the project

The people who know the policies are also running HR. When they can only give the project an hour here and there, the implementation team waits for decisions, and waiting is billed time.

**Fix:** name one decision-maker on your side with protected time, and agree a weekly decision meeting. Decisions that wait more than a week go to a sponsor.

## 6. Customising before trying the standard

Teams often ask the new system to copy the old one: the same approval steps, the same screens, the same reports. Every customisation is code to build, test and maintain through future upgrades.

**Fix:** run phase one on standard features. Customise only what the standard flow genuinely cannot do, and put the rest on a written phase-two list.

## 7. No parallel run and no tracking

Projects that switch payroll over without a parallel run find errors on payday, and fixing them under pressure is expensive. Projects that do not compare spend with budget at each milestone find the overrun only at the end.

**Fix:** run at least one payroll cycle in parallel and reconcile it line by line. Review budget against actuals at every milestone, not at the end.

## A checklist to keep HR and payroll on budget

1. Budget internal effort alongside the partner's work.
2. Start data cleanup in week one, with an owner.
3. List and price every integration before signing.
4. Document payroll rules and exceptions; get payroll to sign them off.
5. Give one decision-maker protected time.
6. Keep phase one on standard features.
7. Run payroll in parallel before switching.
8. Track budget against actuals at each milestone.
9. Hold 10 to 15 percent contingency for genuine surprises.
