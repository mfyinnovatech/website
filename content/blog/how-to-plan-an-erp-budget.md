Most ERP budgets are wrong before the project starts, because they are built from the wrong number. A company asks for a price, receives a licence quote and an implementation estimate, and treats their sum as the budget. Then data, integrations, training and internal time arrive. This guide shows what an ERP budget really needs to cover and how to build one that holds.

## The cost components of an ERP project

| Component | What it covers | Often forgotten? |
|---|---|---|
| Software licences or subscription | Users and apps, per month or per year | No |
| Implementation services | Discovery, configuration, project management | No |
| Data migration | Cleaning, mapping, importing and verifying data | Yes |
| Integrations | Banks, payment gateways, e-commerce, payroll, other systems | Yes |
| Customisation | Features the standard software does not provide | Partly |
| Training | Role-based training before go-live | Yes |
| Internal staff time | Your people's hours on decisions, data and testing | Yes |
| Hosting and infrastructure | Cloud hosting, backups, environments | Sometimes |
| Support and upgrades | Help after go-live, version upgrades, new needs | Yes |

If your budget has only the first two lines, it is not a budget yet.

## What pushes the cost up

- **Scope.** Every process and every company or branch in scope adds configuration, testing and training.
- **Data quality.** Clean, consistent data migrates quickly. Duplicate customers and products with several codes take weeks.
- **Customisation.** Each customisation is built once, then tested and maintained through every upgrade.
- **Integrations.** Each connected system is a small project with its own testing.
- **Decision speed.** Waiting for decisions is paid time. Slow decisions are one of the least visible cost drivers.
- **Local requirements.** Multi-currency, local tax rules, payroll rules and bilingual documents all need configuration. In Kuwait, for example, the dinar's three decimals and Arabic documents must be set up before any transaction is entered.

## Build the budget per phase

Do not budget the whole ERP vision at once. Budget phase one in detail and the later phases as a range.

1. **Write the scope for phase one**: processes, users, integrations, data to migrate, reports, and what done means.
2. **Keep phase one close to standard.** Standard flows are cheaper to configure, test and upgrade.
3. **Price each integration and each customisation separately**, so you can see what each one costs and decide whether it belongs in phase one.
4. **Add internal effort**: hours for your decision-maker, data owners and testers.
5. **Add support for the first months after go-live**, including the first month-end close.
6. **Hold a 10 to 15 percent contingency** for genuine surprises such as data quality or a regulatory change.

## The internal costs people forget

Your own people will spend real time on the project: deciding how processes should work, cleaning data, testing, attending training and running old and new systems in parallel for a while. If the project is staffed by people who also run the business full time, plan for that load, or the timeline will slip and the budget with it.

## Questions to ask every ERP partner before you sign

1. What exactly is included for data migration, and who cleans the data?
2. Which of our requirements are standard, and which need customisation?
3. How is each integration priced and tested?
4. What training is included, and for which roles?
5. What support is included after go-live, and for how long?
6. How are changes handled once the project has started?

## A budget that holds

The projects that stay on budget are not the ones with the lowest estimate. They are the ones with a written scope, a standard phase one, clean data and fast decisions.
